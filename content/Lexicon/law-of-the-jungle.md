> The law of the jungle is commonly used to mean no law at all. Its original meaning was almost the opposite: shared rules that make collective survival possible.

Modern power often presents extraction as nature. The strongest takes what it can, the weak accept their place, and any attempt to impose obligation is dismissed as naive. Calling this arrangement the law of the jungle gives organised self-interest the authority of the natural world.

Rudyard Kipling introduced the phrase in *The Jungle Book* and *The Second Jungle Book*, where the Law of the Jungle is a code taught within the wolf pack. It governs hunting, conduct, protection, and the obligations that bind the group. The strength of the pack depends on the wolf, and the strength of the wolf depends on the pack.

The phrase's modern meaning reverses that structure. It describes a social order in which formal rules may exist but effective power belongs to whoever can impose costs, capture institutions, or escape accountability. The inversion matters because it turns a political choice into an apparent fact of life. A society that treats predation as inevitable stops asking who designed the cage, who benefits from the disorder, and what obligations would make freedom durable.

The actual lesson is more demanding. Collective life requires law, but law is not merely restriction. At its best, it is the shared architecture that prevents strength from becoming predation and allows individual power to contribute to the survival of the whole.

*Sources: Rudyard Kipling, "The Law of the Jungle," in *The Second Jungle Book* (1895); Oxford Reference, "The Jungle Book"; Oxford English Dictionary definition as reproduced in the reference literature.*
