> News media do not simply show the public what happened. They organise which parts of what happened become public reality.

News media are often described as a window onto the world. That metaphor hides the work done by the frame. Reporters, editors, producers, owners, sources, platforms, and audiences all participate in deciding which events become news, how much attention they receive, and what language will be used to make them intelligible.

The institutional role of news media is therefore double. They provide information about events that most people cannot witness directly, while also constructing an agenda of public importance. Limited time, editorial routines, available sources, commercial pressures, and assumptions about newsworthiness shape the resulting map. Prominence, conflict, novelty, timeliness, proximity, and human interest can determine what passes through the gate and what remains invisible.

## Agenda and Frame

Agenda-setting describes the power to make some issues more salient than others. The amount and placement of coverage influence what audiences treat as urgent. Framing goes further by organising the meaning of an issue. It can present the same policy as protection or control, the same protest as civic participation or disorder, and the same economic event as recovery or exploitation.

This does not mean every journalist consciously manufactures a preferred reality. Much of the construction happens through ordinary professional practice. Journalists depend on accessible sources, standard categories, deadlines, institutional expertise, and established story forms. Those routines can reproduce the priorities of powerful institutions because official sources are often easier to reach and more likely to be treated as credible.

The public consequence is that news literacy requires more than checking whether a sentence is factually correct. It requires asking what was selected, who was given authority to speak, which alternatives were excluded, what frame connects the facts, and whose interests become easier to defend as a result.

*Source: McCombs, M.E. & Shaw, D.L. (1972), "The Agenda-Setting Function of Mass Media," Public Opinion Quarterly, 36(2), 176-187; Tuchman, G. (1978), Making News: A Study in the Construction of Reality; Shoemaker, P.J. & Vos, T. (2009), Gatekeeping Theory.*
