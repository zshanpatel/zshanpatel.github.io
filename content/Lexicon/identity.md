> People aren't usually bad at logic. They're protecting something logic can't touch.

When a piece of evidence threatens to put someone at odds with the group they belong to, the evidence tends to lose. Research on identity-protective cognition finds that people — including people who otherwise score well on measures of reasoning ability — selectively credit or dismiss information in whatever pattern best preserves their standing with a group whose beliefs matter to them, and that this effect gets *stronger*, not weaker, among people with more capacity for sophisticated reasoning, since they're better equipped to rationalise the conclusion they already needed.

This is a large part of why simply supplying someone with correct information so often fails to change what they believe: the obstacle usually isn't a lack of facts, it's that accepting the facts would cost something socially.

*Source: Kahan, D.M. (2017). "Misconceptions, Misinformation, and the Logic of Identity-Protective Cognition."*
