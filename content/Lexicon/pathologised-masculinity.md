> A man without a cause is not humble. He is hollow.

The term "toxic masculinity" entered academic circulation through the mythopoetic men's movement of the 1980s and 90s, particularly through Shepherd Bliss, who contrasted stereotypical masculinity with a "deep" or "real" masculinity he claimed men had lost. By 2017, the term had migrated from academic journals into mainstream discourse, policy documents, and school curricula. Carol Harrington's research tracks the shift: before 2011, academic texts referencing "toxic masculinity" never numbered above 20 per year. After 2017, references returned in the thousands.

The critique from within masculinity studies is now substantial. Waling (2019) argues the concept relies on a "diseased" versus "well" body metaphor, treating masculinity itself as a pathology rather than examining specific harmful behaviours. Harrington (2021) contends it "individualises responsibility for gender inequalities to certain bad men." A 2026 ScienceDirect review notes the tendency to define traditional masculinity by its negative traits while ignoring positive traits such as protecting others, displaying courage, and maintaining honour. A 2024 Family Education Trust report states plainly: "It cannot be good science to pathologise half of the human race."

This is what the essay calls pathologised masculinity. Not the academic debate over whether the term is useful, but the lived consequence: a culture that has labelled a man's outward nature, his drive toward greatness, his willingness to sacrifice himself for something greater than his own comfort, as ego and toxicity. The man who seeks a cause is diagnosed. The man who takes risks is reckless. The man who leads is controlling.

The pathologisation works by collapsing the distinction between destructive behaviour and masculine orientation. Aggression is pathological. But the essay is not describing aggression. It is describing directionality: a man's natural orientation toward outward achievement, toward building, protecting, and ultimately sacrificing for something beyond himself. When this orientation is labelled ego, the man does not become humble. He becomes hollow. He loses the cause that gave his discipline, his risk-taking, and his emotional control their meaning.

Raewyn Connell's framework of hegemonic masculinity, the dominant academic lens for understanding men's behaviour, treats masculinity as a social construction maintained through power relations. The essay accepts that culture shapes masculine expression, but rejects the conclusion that masculine traits themselves are pathological. The distinction is critical: cultural corruption of a trait is not the same as the trait being inherently harmful. Pathologising masculinity confuses the two.
