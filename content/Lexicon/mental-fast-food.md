> Mental fast food is information engineered to satisfy attention quickly while leaving understanding hungry.

The modern information environment does not merely contain more material than any person can read. It presents a continuous supply of compressed, emotionally charged fragments that are easy to consume and difficult to metabolise. A headline, clip, notification, hot take, or recommendation arrives with the flavour of importance, then disappears before it has become knowledge.

The food metaphor matters because the problem is not information abundance by itself. Attention is scarce, and abundance gives producers an incentive to make each item more immediately attractive. The result is a stream optimised for the next bite: novelty, outrage, fear, status, and personal relevance. These qualities increase the chance of a click or another minute of viewing. They do not guarantee accuracy, context, reflection, or usefulness.

Mental fast food therefore describes a relationship between content and consumption. A serious book, a careful conversation, and a short video can all carry nourishment. A short item can also be junk. The decisive question is whether the material enlarges the reader's capacity to perceive and act, or simply keeps the appetite for stimulation alive.

The commercial system benefits from the second condition. A person who has learned something may leave the feed and do something with it. A person kept in a state of anticipatory hunger returns for another fragment. The platform receives another unit of attention, while the reader mistakes continued intake for intellectual progress.

An information diet is not solved by refusing every entertaining form. It is solved by recovering choice, sequence, and standards. Read what requires attention. Stay with an idea long enough to test it. Let knowledge change the structure of your judgment rather than merely decorate the next reaction.

*Source: Herbert A. Simon's 1971 account of attention scarcity in an information-rich world is the foundational academic frame. The food metaphor is developed in independent analysis by Ribbonfarm (2008), while Psychology Today (2026) applies the phrase to algorithmically curated, sensationalist information. Citation tier: foundational academic concept plus practitioner and popular psychology sources.*
