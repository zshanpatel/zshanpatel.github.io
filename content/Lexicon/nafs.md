> Not the enemy to be destroyed — the raw material to be disciplined.

Modern psychology's "ego" and Islamic thought's nafs sound like they're pointing at the same thing, and in one specific, deliberate comparison they are — but the comparison exists to show where the two frameworks then diverge, not just where they overlap.

One structured Islamic-psychology reading maps nafs onto Freud's id — the seat of raw desire and instinctual drive — with the *[[Lexicon/qalb|qalb]]* (heart) functioning analogously to Freud's ego, the executive faculty that must manage those desires, and the *[[Lexicon/rooh|rooh]]* (spirit) functioning as a superego that, unlike Freud's, is treated as a source of moral liberation rather than mere restriction. Where the secular Freudian frame treats the ego as the appropriate guiding force and the superego as inherently constraining, this reading inverts the value judgment: the goal isn't for the qalb to dominate unrestrained — that produces individualism, material dependence, and hyper-consumption — but for it to work in harmony with the higher faculty, disciplining the nafs rather than being ruled by it.

This disciplining process is named *tazkiyat al-nafs* — purification of the self — and the broader struggle against the nafs's demands is one sense of *jihad* (etymologically "to strive or struggle," internal as much as external). Desire itself isn't treated as the problem; unregulated desire, and a self-concept organized entirely around satisfying it, is. *Source: grounded in this vault's own research thesis (*The Research Thesis/04-islamic-perspective.md*, drawing the Freudian parallel via Freud, S. (1949)* Group Psychology and the Analysis of the Ego*), within the wider "Islam as complete system" frame set out in Israr Ahmed,* Islamic Renaissance: The Real Task Ahead *(2001).*
