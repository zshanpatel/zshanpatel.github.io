> A social cause is a problem that institutions have decided to address, its boundaries drawn by those with the power to allocate attention and resources.

The designation of a social cause is not neutral. It involves decisions about which problems matter, which are solvable, and which are acceptable costs of the existing order. Climate change becomes a cause while the economic system driving it remains unexamined. Hunger becomes a cause while the distribution systems that produce it remain intact.

The power to designate causes is the power to set the terms of intervention. When institutions define what counts as a social problem, they simultaneously define what does not. Structural inequality, systemic oppression, and economic hierarchy are rarely designated as causes because addressing them would require transforming the systems that designate causes in the first place.

The language of social cause serves to channel legitimate grievance into manageable frameworks. Activism becomes programme design. Protest becomes proposal writing. Transformation becomes project management. The radical potential of addressing root causes is converted into the conservative practice of treating symptoms within existing structures.
