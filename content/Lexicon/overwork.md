> Overwork is the condition of consistently labouring beyond sustainable limits, normalised as ambition while functioning as exploitation.

The glorification of overwork serves specific economic interests. When excessive labour is framed as dedication, ambition, or hustle, it individualises what is structurally imposed. The worker who stays late is not exploited but committed. The employee who answers emails at midnight is not abused but passionate. This framing converts systemic extraction into personal virtue.

Overwork produces predictable outcomes: burnout, health deterioration, relationship breakdown, and the erosion of the capacity to organise or resist. A workforce that is perpetually exhausted has no energy for collective action. Overwork functions as a control mechanism, keeping workers too tired to think clearly about their conditions.

The distribution of overwork is not random. It follows existing hierarchies. Those with less power, fewer alternatives, and weaker institutional protections bear disproportionate burdens. The ability to set boundaries, to say no, to work reasonable hours is itself a form of privilege that concentrates along lines of class, race, and social capital.
