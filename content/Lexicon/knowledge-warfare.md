> Knowledge warfare is the systematic attack on language to control what is acceptable, using labels to disarm positions without ever engaging their substance.

Language is not a neutral medium through which ideas pass. It is the terrain on which ideas live or die. When you control the vocabulary available to describe a position, you do not need to refute that position. You only need to make it unsayable. This is the core mechanism of knowledge warfare: not the suppression of an idea through counter-argument, but the corruption of the words that would be needed to express it.

The tactic is older than any single political moment, but the academic study of it has sharpened in recent decades. Celine-Marie Pascale's work on the weaponization of language describes how authoritarian systems use "strategic and systematic relationships between symbolic and material forms of violence" to control discourse, deploying propaganda, disinformation, censorship, and the mundane corruption of everyday language as interlocking tools. Language, once weaponised, does not need to be enforced at gunpoint. It self-propagates through social imitation. People repeat the corrupted terms because everyone else is using them, and the repetition normalises the distortion until it becomes invisible.

In the context of religious discourse, the mechanism works with particular efficiency. A word like "extremist" performs a specific function: it locates a position outside the boundary of acceptable thought without ever examining the substance of that position. It is a descriptive label deployed as a disqualifier. The moment someone is called an extremist for articulating a religious position, the conversation shifts from evaluating the position to evaluating the speaker's sanity and social acceptability. The idea has not been countered. It has been made dangerous to engage with. And everyone watching learns the lesson: there are positions you are not allowed to hold, not because they are wrong, but because holding them will cost you social standing.

This is knowledge warfare operating at the level of inference. As Lynne Tirrell's work on toxic speech describes, language can be injected with "inferential venom" that changes how any utterance is received, regardless of the speaker's intention. Once a term like "extremist" attaches to a category of speech, every future statement in that category arrives pre-discredited. The label does the work that arguments used to do.
