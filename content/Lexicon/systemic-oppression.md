> Systemic oppression is organised disadvantage reproduced by institutions, norms, and relationships across time.

Individual prejudice is often treated as the whole problem because it is easier to identify and punish. That focus can leave the machinery that keeps producing unequal outcomes intact. A system can disadvantage a group through rules, habits, categories, and inherited distributions even when no individual actor openly states an intention to harm it.

Systemic oppression names the historical and organised patterns through which institutions and social structures restrict some groups while protecting or elevating others. It can operate through government, education, work, housing, health, culture, and law. Its force does not depend on one dramatic act. It accumulates through policies, institutional biases, social norms, interpersonal relationships, and the unequal access to resources and authority that those arrangements create.

The term does not mean that every unequal outcome proves oppression, or that individual agency disappears. It identifies a level of causation that individual moral language cannot capture. The diagnostic question is not only who behaved badly, but which arrangements make the same burden predictable, normal, and difficult to escape. Because these arrangements were made by people, they can also be changed by organised recognition and action.

*Source: University of Washington School of Social Work, "Oppression Definition"; Iris Marion Young, "Five Faces of Oppression," as summarised by the University of Michigan School of Social Work.*
