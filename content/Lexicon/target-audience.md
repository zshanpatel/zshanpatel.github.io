> A target audience is not who an advertisement reaches. It is who the advertiser has decided is worth reaching.

Every advertisement excludes more people than it addresses. Before a message is made, the market is divided into groups judged more or less likely to notice, desire, trust, and buy. The target audience is the selected group at the centre of that decision. It is a commercial category built from research, not a natural tribe waiting to be discovered.

Targeting can use demographic and geographic data, but the sharper distinctions usually come from behaviour, lifestyle, values, interests, and motivation. Two people of the same age and income may respond to entirely different messages because one is seeking status, another convenience, and another relief from anxiety. Market research, interviews, surveys, focus groups, and observed purchasing data are used to make these differences actionable.

That classification gives advertising its precision. The message can be tailored to the audience's language, aspiration, insecurity, and preferred channel. The same precision can also turn identity into a handle. A person is no longer approached as a whole human being, but as a bundle of traits associated with a predicted response. Targeting therefore sits close to [[manipulation|manipulation]]: the category itself is not necessarily abusive, but it creates the map through which influence can be delivered.

The target audience is also a story the advertiser tells about the consumer. It selects which parts of a person's life matter to the sale and discards the rest. When the map is good, the message feels uncannily relevant. When the map becomes the only reality, the audience becomes easier to reach and easier to reduce.

*Source: EBSCO Research Starters, "Target Market"; Harvard Business School Online, "The Importance of Target Audience in Marketing Strategy." Institutional and practitioner reference sources, not peer-reviewed journal articles.*
