> Systemic corruption is corruption embedded in institutional structures and norms, reproducing injustice as a normal feature of how systems operate rather than as an aberration from them.

Individual corruption is visible and punishable: the official who takes a bribe, the executive who falsifies accounts, the politician who enriches themselves. Systemic corruption is structural: the arrangements that distribute opportunity unequally, the incentives that reward extraction over production, the norms that normalise advantage for some and exclusion for others.

The distinction matters because individual corruption can be addressed through enforcement, while systemic corruption requires transformation. Removing a corrupt official changes the person, not the system. The system continues to produce the conditions that incentivise corruption. New officials operate within the same structures, face the same incentives, and produce the same outcomes.

Systemic corruption is not a deviation from legitimate systems but a feature of systems that concentrate power without accountability. When those who define the rules benefit from the rules, when those who measure success set the metrics, when those who allocate resources determine the criteria for allocation, corruption is not an aberration. It is the system functioning as designed.
