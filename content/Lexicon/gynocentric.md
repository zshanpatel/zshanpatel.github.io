> A gynocentric system takes the feminine perspective as the social default, penalising male assertiveness as aggression while rewarding emotional performance over direction.

Modern social systems increasingly organise around feminine psychological patterns. Male assertiveness is routinely labelled aggression. Emotional display by men outpaces that of women. Men are dismissed as demanding for holding standards. The result is a society that punishes direction and rewards reactivity.

In evolutionary biology, gynocentrism refers to social organisations centred on female reproductive strategy and female choice. Robert Trivers' parental investment theory (1972) established that the sex investing more per offspring becomes the limiting resource for the other sex, structuring competition and choosiness asymmetrically. Patricia Adair Gowaty's Feminism and Evolutionary Biology (1997) further examined how female strategies shape social organisation within this framework. The term entered broader usage through these debates about whether social structures reflect evolved sex-specific strategies.

The gynocentric dynamic produces a specific failure mode: conversation without conclusion, analysis without decision, emotion without direction. It is a system where nobody defines what matters or who leads, and all parties oscillate between pacification and reaction. The masculine frame that once contained and directed this energy is dissolved, leaving only the oscillation itself.
