> Peripheral receptivity is openness to influence before the substance of a message has been seriously examined.

People do not evaluate every message with the same attention. Fatigue, distraction, low personal relevance, limited knowledge, and time pressure reduce the effort available for checking an argument. In that condition, the surrounding signals become more influential: who delivered the message, how familiar the brand feels, whether the music is attractive, how many people appear to approve, or whether the message produces a quick emotional response.

The elaboration likelihood model distinguishes this low-effort pattern from central-route processing. When motivation and ability are high, people are more likely to scrutinise the quality of the argument. When either is missing, peripheral cues play a larger role in shaping attitudes. The same feature can serve different functions depending on the situation. Expertise may be evidence for someone who checks the claim, or merely a shortcut for someone who does not.

Peripheral receptivity is not proof of stupidity or permanent gullibility. It is a normal response to limited attention, and every person enters it repeatedly. The vulnerability appears when institutions deliberately arrange the environment so that audiences stay distracted, emotionally activated, or too hurried to examine what is being offered. Influence then arrives through the conditions of reception as much as through the message itself.

The practical defence is not suspicion of every feeling or rejection of every image, sound, or credible source. It is the deliberate restoration of evaluation at the moment a cue begins to move behaviour. Asking what is being claimed, who benefits, and whether the response would survive a slower reading turns a peripheral reaction back into a considered judgment.

*Source: Petty, R.E. & Cacioppo, J.T. (1986). "The Elaboration Likelihood Model of Persuasion." Advances in Experimental Social Psychology, 19, 123-205. DOI: 10.1016/S0065-2601(08)60214-2.*
