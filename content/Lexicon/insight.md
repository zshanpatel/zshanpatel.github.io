> Insight is not more data. It is the explanation that makes behaviour intelligible.

The modern organisation can collect almost endless information about people and still fail to understand them. A dashboard can show what someone clicked, bought, watched, or abandoned. It cannot by itself explain the fear, aspiration, frustration, identity, or social pressure that made the action meaningful.

Insight is the interpretive step between observation and action. It identifies a pattern in behaviour, then explains the human motive or cultural condition behind that pattern. In marketing, a useful insight does not merely describe an audience as "young professionals" or "price sensitive." It shows the tension they are trying to resolve, such as wanting status without appearing materialistic, or wanting control while feeling overwhelmed by choice.

This is why insight is more valuable than raw audience data. Data can segment a population. Insight can reveal the sentence a person is unable or unwilling to say directly about their own life. That discovery may help a brand create a genuinely relevant product or message, but it can also become the intelligence used to locate a person's vulnerability and press on it.

Consumer-insight practice commonly combines interviews, surveys, behavioural data, ethnography, and social listening. None of these methods produces insight automatically. The work is the disciplined interpretation of evidence, with enough cultural and psychological understanding to distinguish a durable human tension from a temporary correlation.

*Source: Michon Creative, "Why consumer insights are so important in marketing" (industry source, accessed via web research).*
