> The person who used to get laughed out of the room for saying "I have a great idea" — now the one thing that's hardest to automate.

In 1949, three copywriters left Grey Advertising to found Doyle Dane Bernbach on a bet that advertising was an art, not a science — that a genuinely strange idea, argued for against the grain of an agency's own account executives, was worth more than technically polished, conventional work. Bill Bernbach's "Think Small" campaign for Volkswagen and "We Try Harder" for Avis weren't executional triumphs; they were bets on outsider thinking and refusal to conform to a category's own rules — the actual substance of what a Creative Revolution-era creative director was paid to supply.

Decades later, tech culture inverted the same figure into an insult. In startup lore, the "idea guy" is the founder who shows up with a grand vision and waves away the "implementation details," because in software, ideas were cheap and execution — the actual writing of working code — was the genuinely scarce skill. The role that had been the entire point of the job in advertising became, in engineering, the thing you got mocked for not being able to do yourself.

AI is now doing to that second inversion what Bernbach did to 1950s advertising: commoditising the execution half of the equation. As technical execution stops being the bottleneck, the older meaning of the term — someone whose scarce contribution is the idea itself, argued from outside convention — is reasserting itself. Same instinct, same non-conformism, same willingness to be the outcast in the room; only the industry doing the mocking has changed.
