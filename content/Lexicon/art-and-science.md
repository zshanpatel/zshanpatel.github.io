> The split felt natural for about a century. It isn't — it's a specific, dateable diagnosis of what happened to Western education.

In 1959, physicist-and-novelist C.P. Snow delivered the Rede Lecture that gave this divide its name: "The Two Cultures," describing a "gulf of mutual incomprehension" between scientists and literary intellectuals who could no longer talk to each other, each side treating basic fluency in the other's domain as unnecessary. Snow's point wasn't that the split was good or inevitable — it was a warning about the cost of two educated classes who'd stopped being able to communicate.

The split he diagnosed has since become the default assumption: that "the arts" and "the sciences" are naturally separate tracks requiring separate temperaments. Before the split hardened, they weren't seen that way — the Renaissance polymath and the classical *[[ilm|('ilm)]]* tradition both treated inquiry as one continuous activity, not two. "Art and science weren't separate, they informed each other" is a return to the older framing, not a new one.

Source: Snow, C.P. *The Two Cultures*, Rede Lecture, 1959; Cohen, B. "Science and humanities: across two cultures and into science studies," *Endeavour*, 2001.
