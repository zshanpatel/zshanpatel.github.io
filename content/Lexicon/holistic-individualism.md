> Not a movement, not a school. A claim: that a person's flourishing and their community's flourishing rise or fall together, and cannot be traded off one against the other.

Debates about the individual and the collective are usually staged as a forced choice. One side argues that maximizing individual liberty, effort, and reward is what ultimately lifts everyone, so the community's job is to get out of the way. The other argues that the group is the primary unit that matters, so individual interest should bend to collective need. Both framings assume a trade-off exists at the point of contact between a person and their community. Holistic individualism denies that assumption. It holds that individual prosperity pursued in isolation from the community's health is ultimately hollow, and that community prosperity built without room for individual development is ultimately unsustainable, so treating the two as opposed misdescribes the relationship rather than resolving it.

A note on the term itself: "holistic individualism" is not a standard, independently sourced phrase with its own body of scholarship. It functions here as a label for a real and long-studied tension, borrowed and combined from adjacent literatures rather than inherited from one of them. Readers encountering the phrase elsewhere should not assume a shared canonical definition exists.

The closest established academic anchor is communitarianism, particularly the "responsive communitarianism" associated with sociologist Amitai Etzioni. Etzioni's *The Spirit of Community* (1993) argues explicitly against treating individual rights and community responsibility as competing claims where one side must win. Responsive communitarians hold that neither the common good nor individual rights is automatically privileged over the other; each society has to work out its own balance, and strong individual rights presuppose strong social responsibilities rather than existing independently of them. This is the same structural move holistic individualism makes: not "collective over individual" or "individual over collective," but a refusal to accept that the two sit on opposite ends of one scale.

A parallel case for the same refusal appears in the philosophy of social science, in what physicist and philosopher Mario Bunge called systemism. Social theory has long argued over whether individuals or social wholes are the more basic unit of explanation: methodological individualism reduces society to individual actions, methodological holism treats social structures as having their own irreducible logic. Systemism proposes a third position: individuals and the relations connecting them into a system are jointly constitutive, and neither is reducible to the other. A person without relationships is an abstraction; a social structure without the individuals composing and altering it is empty. Holistic individualism applies this same non-reductive move to human flourishing rather than to social explanation.

Islamic legal and social philosophy offers a working example of the same structure rather than a proof of it. Classical jurisprudence distinguishes *fard al-kifayah*, a communal obligation (feeding the hungry, providing medical care, and similar needs) that is discharged once enough individuals within the community take it up, from *fard al-ayn*, a duty owed by every individual regardless of what anyone else does. If the community fails to meet a communal obligation collectively, it reverts to being every individual's personal duty. This same interlocking logic, individual action and collective outcome bound together rather than opposed, sits within the broader legal category of *[[maslahah|(maslahah)]]*, public interest, which Islamic law treats as a standing consideration in its own right rather than a byproduct of aggregated individual choices.
