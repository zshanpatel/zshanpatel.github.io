> A Cambridge- and Munich-trained philosopher who devoted his career to a paradox: that reviving Islam meant restoring the very intellectual audacity centuries of scholars had trained Muslims to distrust.

By the early twentieth century, much of the Islamic world had inherited a body of religious law widely treated as closed. Centuries of taqlid, imitation of past legal rulings rather than fresh reasoning from first principles, had left Muslim scholarship largely reproducing precedent rather than confronting new circumstances, at precisely the moment European colonial power was reorganising the same societies by force. Muhammad Iqbal, a poet trained in Western academic philosophy, identified this intellectual immobility, not military or economic weakness on its own, as the deeper crisis, and spent his career building a philosophical case for reopening it.

Muhammad Iqbal (1877-1938) was born in Sialkot, Punjab, British India, and educated at Government College, Lahore, before earning a further BA at Trinity College, Cambridge, qualifying as a barrister at Lincoln's Inn, and completing a PhD in philosophy at Ludwig Maximilian University of Munich (1907) with a dissertation on the development of metaphysics in Persia. He returned to Lahore to practice law and teach philosophy, and published across a body of poetry, mostly in Persian and Urdu, that ran to roughly 12,000 verses. He was knighted in 1922 and is honoured in Pakistan as Allama ("the learned") and as Mufakkir-e-Pakistan, "the Thinker of Pakistan."

## Khudi: Dynamic Selfhood

His long philosophical poem *Asrar-i-Khudi* (*Secrets of the Self*, 1915) introduced *khudi*, ordinarily just the Urdu word for "self" or "ego," as a technical concept: the self as a dynamic, striving force whose development toward greater individuality and self-realisation Iqbal treated as a good in itself, not an obstacle to spiritual life. He rejected the strands of Sufi thought that treated self-effacement (*fana*) as the highest goal, arguing instead that a Muslim's task was to strengthen and assert selfhood through struggle, initiative and even productive suffering, not dissolve it into passivity. The 1917 sequel, *Rumuz-i-Bekhudi* (*Secrets of Selflessness*), balanced this individualism against the self's obligations to community, arguing that a fully realised self only reaches completion within the collective life of the *[[ummah|ummah]]*.

## Ijtihad as the "Principle of Movement"

Iqbal's most sustained philosophical argument appears in *The Reconstruction of Religious Thought in Islam* (1930), a set of lectures delivered in Madras, Hyderabad and Aligarh that attempt to restate Islamic theology in terms responsive to modern philosophy and science. Its central claim is that classical Islamic law had, over centuries, effectively closed the gate of *ijtihad*, independent legal and theological reasoning, freezing jurisprudence at something close to a medieval snapshot. Iqbal called ijtihad "the principle of movement in the structure of Islam," arguing it was never meant to be permanently suspended, and that a religion claiming to guide changing human societies required a built-in mechanism for continual reinterpretation rather than perpetual deference to past rulings. This was not a call to discard revelation for unmoored reasoning: Iqbal held that rightly used reason and revelation converge, and that a revived, empirically engaged ijtihad was the corrective to what he saw as centuries of interpretive paralysis, not a rejection of the tradition itself.

## Political Legacy

Iqbal served on the Punjab Legislative Council and as president of the Punjab Muslim League. In his 1930 presidential address at Allahabad, he proposed consolidating India's Muslim-majority northwestern provinces into a single, self-governing state, an idea historians treat as a direct conceptual precursor to Pakistan, although Iqbal died in 1938, nine years before the country's actual creation, and never used the name "Pakistan" in his own writing. His correspondence with Muhammad Ali Jinnah in his final years is credited with helping move Jinnah toward the demand for partition. He is Pakistan's official national poet, and his tomb sits at the entrance to Lahore's Badshahi Mosque.
