> Being creative in painting doesn't make you creative in mathematics. The research on this is more surprising than the compliment "you're so creative" suggests.

It's tempting to treat creativity as a single trait some people have and others don't — a general-purpose spark that shows up wherever it's applied. The empirical picture is messier: creative achievement clusters by domain. People who accomplish creative work in the visual arts don't reliably show the same pattern in writing, science, or performance; the "uncreative" and domain-specific classes are statistically more common than the all-purpose creative genius.

That matters for how this essay uses the term. "Creative fields" isn't shorthand for "the fun, unstructured ones", it names a real, empirically distinct way of working, one where the goal is genuine novelty rather than correct execution of a known procedure, and where skill in one domain doesn't automatically transfer to another.

Source: Silvia, P.J., Kaufman, J.C., & Pretz, J.E. "Is creativity domain-specific? Latent class models of creative accomplishments and creative self-descriptions," *Psychology of Aesthetics, Creativity, and the Arts*, 2009.
