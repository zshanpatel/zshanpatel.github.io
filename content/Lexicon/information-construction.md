> Information is never only transmitted. It is selected, arranged, and made salient before anyone receives it.

The common picture of information is that reality happens first and a neutral message reports it afterward. In practice, every message begins with decisions about what to include, what to leave out, where to begin, which words to use, and what to make prominent. Those decisions construct the version of reality that an audience can see.

Information construction does not require fabrication. A report can contain accurate facts and still produce a partial or distorted understanding because the facts have been selected and connected inside a particular frame. The same event can be presented as a failure of individuals, a failure of institutions, a market problem, or a moral crisis. Each description directs attention toward different causes, responsibilities, and possible responses.

## Selection Becomes Meaning

Selection determines salience. Repetition, emotional intensity, novelty, conflict, and personal relevance make some details easier to notice and remember than others. Ordering then gives those details a relationship. A headline can make one fact feel like the cause, a photograph can make one person stand for a whole group, and a statistic can appear decisive when the comparison that gives it meaning has been omitted.

Framing is the interpretive layer of this process. It defines a problem, identifies causes, evaluates responsibility, and makes some solutions appear more reasonable than others. This is why information construction is not merely a question of accuracy. It is also a question of architecture: who gets to define the categories through which accuracy will be judged.

The result is not always manipulation. Construction is unavoidable because no communication can contain everything. The ethical question is whether the construction is transparent, proportionate, and open to correction, or whether it quietly narrows the audience's field of judgment while presenting its own frame as reality itself.

*Source: Tuchman, G. (1978), Making News: A Study in the Construction of Reality; Entman, R.M. (1993), "Framing: Toward Clarification of a Fractured Paradigm," Journal of Communication, 43(4), 51-58.*
