> A claim you're invited to test, not just accept.

Most sacred texts ask for belief in what they report — historical events, moral commands, metaphysical claims that can't be independently checked. I'jaz makes a different kind of claim: that the text itself, as a literary and structural object, is something no comparable human effort has managed to reproduce — a claim about the artifact, not just its contents, and one its proponents present as open to attempted refutation.

The doctrine took roughly two centuries after the Qur'an's revelation to become a subject of sustained scholarly attention, developing partly in response to the rise of Sufism, Mu'tazili theology, and Muslim-Christian polemical exchange in the 9th century. Its most influential classical formulation is Abd al-Qahir al-Jurjani's 11th-century *Dala'il al-I'jaz* ("The Proofs of Inimitability"), which grounded the claim in the Qur'an's precise use of syntax and word order rather than in vague appeals to beauty. Arguments for i'jaz have historically clustered around several axes: linguistic and literary inimitability, internal consistency across a text revealed piecemeal over 23 years, and — more contested — claims of predictive or scientific foreknowledge.

The doctrine remains an active subject of academic study rather than a closed medieval debate — recent scholarship continues to reassess its dimensions and how it functions in modern religious discourse. *Source: M.Q. Akhoondzada, "The Inimitability (I'jaz) of the Glorious Qur'an," Journal of Social Sciences, Kabul University, 5(1) (2025), 131–147 — a regional peer-reviewed venue, noted honestly rather than as a top-tier journal.*
