> Meritocracy is the belief that talent and effort, rather than birth or connections, should determine social position, functioning as both aspiration and justification for existing hierarchies.

The meritocratic ideal holds that anyone can rise through talent and hard work. This is attractive because it promises fairness: outcomes reflect inputs, success is earned, failure is deserved. The problem is that meritocracy describes how modern societies justify inequality more than how they actually produce it.

In practice, merit operates within a field of pre-existing advantages. Access to education, nutrition, healthcare, networks, mentorship, and capital all shape who develops merit and who can convert it into advancement. The child of wealthy parents attends better schools, receives more support, and enters job markets with connections that no amount of individual talent can replicate. When the race starts at different points, declaring it fair based on who finishes first is a description of the starting line, not the race.

Meritocracy serves as ideology by converting structural inequality into personal virtue. The successful deserve their success because they earned it. The unsuccessful deserve their position because they didn't try hard enough. This framing makes inequality appear natural and just, obscuring the systems that distribute opportunity unevenly from the start.
