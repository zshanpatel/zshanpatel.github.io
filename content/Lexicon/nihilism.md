> A life without meaning is not a tragedy you survive. It is a jail you never notice.

The modern crisis is not dramatic collapse but quiet drift. People fill their days with routine, consumption, and low-grade stimulation, never arriving at anything, never reaching a point that forces them to confront what they are doing or why. The erosion happens fraction by fraction: ambition thins, attention scatters, and the capacity for genuine orientation toward anything dissolves so gradually that the absence goes unremarked. This is the problem nihilism diagnoses. It is not a philosophy anyone chooses. It is the condition that settles in when meaning was never actively constructed, and the structures that once supplied it have quietly ceased to function.

Nihilism, from the Latin *nihil* (nothing), entered modern philosophy through Friedrich Jacobi's 1799 critique of Kantian idealism, then entered popular culture through Ivan Turgenev's 1862 novel *Fathers and Sons*, where it described a generation that rejected all inherited values without offering replacements in return. Friedrich Nietzsche later made it the central problem of Western modernity: once God, tradition, and metaphysical certainty lose their grip on a civilisation, the values they sustained collapse with them. Nietzsche distinguished passive nihilism, the exhaustion and withdrawal that follows from this collapse, from active nihilism, the clearing-away that precedes the creation of new values. His famous madman parable in *The Gay Science* announces the "death of God" not as a triumph but as a catastrophe, one whose full consequences humanity had not yet grasped.

Existential nihilism, the most common modern usage, is the view that life has no intrinsic meaning or purpose, and that all action is ultimately pointless. Jean-Paul Sartre's formulation that "existence precedes essence" rules out any predetermined human nature or cosmic assignment, leaving each person alone with the task of inventing significance in a universe that offers none. Albert Camus treated the confrontation between the human need for meaning and the universe's silence as the fundamental absurdity, a tension to be lived within rather than resolved. But the passive form, the slow spiral into indifference and comfortable numbness, may be the more dangerous variant. It does not announce itself. It does not feel like a philosophy. It feels like Tuesday.
