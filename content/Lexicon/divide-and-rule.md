> Divide a population along any axis and you have neutralised its capacity to resist. The family is the most consequential axis of all.

Divide and rule (*divide et impera*) is one of the oldest documented strategies of imperial control. Tradition attributes its origin to Philip II of Macedon (382-336 BCE), and it was systematically employed by Julius Caesar during the conquest of Gaul, where he allied with individual Gallic tribes against their traditional rivals, ensuring he never faced a unified opposition (*Commentarii de Bello Gallico*, Book II). The British Raj formalised the strategy across India, with Lord Elphinstone declaring that "divide et impera was the old Roman maxim, and it should be ours."

The strategy operates through four steps, as identified by Richard Morrock: the manufacture of differences within a targeted population, the amplification of existing differences, the use of these differences for imperial benefit, and the carry-over of these differences into the post-colonial period. When both parents chase careers under the promise of liberation, the result is double taxation, double consumption, and double debt. The family unit fractures into two competing individual consumers rather than functioning as a cooperative economic and spiritual household.

The modern application is invisible precisely because it is framed as freedom. No external power forces the division. The population divides itself, pursuing individual economic goals while the institutional architecture extracts value from every additional worker, borrower, and consumer. The family ceases to be a unit of collective resilience and becomes another node in the economic machine.
