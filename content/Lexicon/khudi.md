> The Self as a force to be strengthened, not a self to be dissolved.

For centuries, a strand of Sufi poetry taught that spiritual perfection meant erasing the self: extinguishing desire, ambition, and individual will until the "drop" disappeared into the "ocean" of the divine. The poet-philosopher *[[muhammad-iqbal|Muhammad Iqbal]]* (1877-1938) argued that this reading had calcified into a quietism that left the Muslim world passive at the exact moment it needed initiative.

Khudi *[[khudi|(khudi)]]*, the Persian and Urdu word for selfhood or ego, is his answer, developed as the central idea of his 1915 Persian poem *Asrar-i-Khudi* (translated into English by R. A. Nicholson as *The Secrets of the Self*, 1920). For Iqbal, khudi is not a fixed possession but a dynamic force, an ego that grows stronger or weaker depending on how it is used. Love, desire, and struggle strengthen it; dependency, imitation, and begging weaken it. The poem traces a person's development through three stages, obedience to law, self-mastery, and finally divine vicegerency, the point at which a fully strengthened self becomes capable of shaping reality rather than merely enduring it.

The philosophical stakes are explicit. Where classical Sufi metaphysics prized *fana*, self-annihilation before the divine, Iqbal argued for *baqa*, self-affirmation. He held that a fully developed khudi does not dissolve even in the most intense mystical encounter with God; if anything, it becomes more distinctly itself. Annemarie Schimmel, one of the most influential Western interpreters of Iqbal's thought, read this not as an outright rejection of *fana* but as a redefinition of it: Iqbal treated self-annihilation as the shedding of merely human qualities and their replacement with divine ones, so a person becomes unique precisely by growing closer to a uniquely singular God, rather than dissolving into an undifferentiated whole. Iqbal also drew directly on the Qur'anic idea of *[[rooh|rooh]]*, at points using it as near-synonymous with khudi, the animating spark that makes each self a genuine agent rather than an accident of matter.

Critics in Iqbal's own lifetime noted a resemblance between this argument and Nietzsche's will to power, and Iqbal was reading Nietzsche while writing *Asrar-i-Khudi*. He rejected the comparison as a source in a letter to Nicholson, insisting his philosophy grew out of "the experience and speculation of old Muslim Sufis and thinkers." The distinction that mattered most to him was moral rather than genealogical: a strengthened khudi discovers the sources of law within itself instead of placing itself above the law the way he read Nietzsche's superman as doing.

*Source: Muhammad Iqbal, Asrar-i-Khudi, translated by R. A. Nicholson as The Secrets of the Self (1920), including Iqbal's prefatory letter to Nicholson; Annemarie Schimmel, Gabriel's Wing: A Study into the Religious Ideas of Sir Muhammad Iqbal (Brill, 1963).*
