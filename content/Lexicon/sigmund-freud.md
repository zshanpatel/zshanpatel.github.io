> The psyche is not one unified will, but three competing forces locked in constant negotiation.

Modern psychology inherited from Freud a model of internal division: the idea that a person is not simply one thing deciding, wanting, and acting, but rather a system where raw desire, rational management, and internalised authority are perpetually at odds. This diagnosis proved influential precisely because it names a lived experience many recognize: the feeling of being pulled in multiple directions, of wanting something your values forbid, of having to manage impulses that seem to come from outside your conscious control.

Freud's structural model identifies three layers of the psyche. The *id* is the most primitive: the reservoir of instinctual drive and raw desire, operating according to what he called the pleasure principle, demanding immediate gratification without regard for consequence or morality. The *ego* is the executive faculty, the part that engages with reality, delays gratification, and navigates the external world. The *superego* is the internalized moral authority, the voice of conscience, shaped by parental figures and cultural prohibitions. In Freud's account, a stable psyche is one where the ego successfully mediates between the id's demands and the superego's restrictions, neither allowing the id to run unchecked nor being crushed beneath superego pressure.

This framework has proven durable partly because it names real structures in human experience: the fact that we have desires, reasoning, and conscience is evident. Yet the model's universality is contested. Alternative psychological traditions, particularly Islamic psychology, retain similar tripartite structures but invert their hierarchy and function. Where Freud treats the superego as inherently restrictive, other systems, such as the mapping of *[[Lexicon/nafs|nafs]]* (lower self), *[[Lexicon/qalb|qalb]]* (heart), and *[[Lexicon/rooh|rooh]]* (spirit), reimagine the highest faculty not as constraint but as liberation, and the goal not as ego dominance but as harmonious integration under higher authority.

*Source: Freud, S. (1949). Group Psychology and the Analysis of the Ego.*
