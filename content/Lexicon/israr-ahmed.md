> A Lahore physician who gave up his medical practice at thirty-nine to spend the rest of his life arguing, verse by verse, that Islam was never meant to be practised as a private faith.

In much of twentieth-century Pakistani religious life, "Islam" functioned largely as personal piety and ritual observance, layered on top of a legal, economic and political order inherited largely intact from colonial administration. Israr Ahmed treated that arrangement, Islam confined to mosque and home while secular structures ran the state and economy, as itself the central problem, and built a decades-long project of Qur'anic teaching and organising around the claim that Islam contained no such division to begin with.

Israr Ahmed (1932-2010) was born in Hisar, in pre-partition Punjab, and migrated to Pakistan in 1947. He trained as a physician, earning an MBBS from King Edward Medical College, Lahore, in 1954, before separately completing a BA and MA in Islamic Studies at the University of Karachi in 1965. As a student he was active first in the Islami Jamiat-e-Talaba and then joined Abul A'la Maududi's Jamaat-e-Islami, but left the party in the mid-1950s over its decision to compete in electoral politics, a strategy he considered incompatible with the slower, popular re-Islamisation he believed had to precede any political transformation. He continued practising medicine while teaching and writing until 1971, when he gave up his practice to pursue religious work full time. In 1972 he founded Markazi Anjuman Khuddam-ul-Qur'an, an organisation centred on Qur'anic study, and in 1975 organised its activist wing into Tanzeem-e-Islami, which he led as its first Ameer (leader) until 2002. He served one term in Pakistan's National Assembly (1981-1982) under General Zia-ul-Haq and was awarded the Sitara-i-Imtiaz, one of Pakistan's highest civilian honours, the same year.

## Islam as a Complete System

The organising claim across Ahmed's work, most concisely stated in the English-translated monograph *Islamic Renaissance: The Real Task Ahead* (the material was first serialised in Urdu in 1967 and issued as a booklet the following year, with English editions following later), is that Islam is not a religion in the modern, privatised Western sense at all, but a *[[beyond-religion|deen]]*, a total, self-contained system integrating belief and worship with economic, legal, social and political organisation. On this reading, treating Islam as a matter of individual conscience while leaving law, finance and governance to structures inherited from colonial rule was not a neutral arrangement but a specific and consequential narrowing of the religion, and reversing it required more than personal reform: it required the eventual re-establishment of an Islamic political order, which Ahmed argued should take the form of a revived khilafah (caliphate), with Pakistan as its starting point rather than the historical Arab centres of the caliphate.

## Tafsir and Method

Ahmed's most lasting public legacy is arguably pedagogical rather than institutional. Over roughly three decades he delivered *Bayan-ul-Qur'an*, a verse-by-verse Urdu exegesis of the entire Qur'an, alongside a companion Arabic-grammar course, *Dawra-e-Tarjuma Qur'an*, designed to let ordinary Urdu speakers read the text directly rather than relying solely on scholars' translations. Recordings of both circulated widely on cassette, satellite television and later YouTube, reaching an audience well beyond Tanzeem-e-Islami's formal membership and making him one of the most widely heard Qur'an teachers in the Urdu-speaking world of his generation. He wrote roughly sixty books and monographs in Urdu, of which around thirty had been translated into English and other languages by the time of his death.

## Reception

Ahmed's legacy is contested rather than uniformly celebrated. Supporters credit him with a distinctive synthesis of the revivalist tradition associated with Maududi, the earlier philosophical case for renewal made by Muhammad Iqbal, and independent Qur'anic scholarship. Critics, including the scholar Vali Nasr, have linked some of his 1980s activity to Saudi-funded sectarian campaigning against Pakistan's Shia minority, and other researchers have noted that recordings of his lectures on Islamic revolution have circulated within some jihadist networks, a use Tanzeem-e-Islami itself, which describes its own methodology as non-violent and gradualist, explicitly disavows.

Most of the accessible English-language material on Ahmed comes from Tanzeem-e-Islami's own publications and general encyclopaedic sources rather than peer-reviewed academic scholarship, which remains thinner on him in English than on contemporaries like Maududi. That should be read as a gap in the English-language secondary literature, not as a comment on the substance of his teaching.
