> Just systems are organisational frameworks that ground distribution, authority, and obligation in principles transcending human preference, ensuring that power is subject to standards it did not create.

The concept of just systems presupposes that systems can be evaluated against standards external to themselves. A system that defines its own metrics of success cannot be judged by those metrics to be unjust. Just systems require a standpoint outside the system, whether divine law, natural law, or rational principles, from which the system's outcomes can be evaluated.

Islamic tradition claims to have established just systems: frameworks for economic exchange, governance, and social organisation grounded in divine revelation. These systems did not eliminate inequality, but they structured it according to principles that constrained the concentration of power. The prohibition on interest, the obligation of charity, the requirement of consultation, and the accountability of rulers all functioned as structural constraints on extraction.

The loss of these systems is presented in the essay as a historical fact: Muslim societies once operated under frameworks that constrained power according to divine standards. The corruption of those systems, and their replacement by technocratic and capitalist alternatives, represents the loss of the external standpoint from which existing hierarchies could be judged and constrained.
