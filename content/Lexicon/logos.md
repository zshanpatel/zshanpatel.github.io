> Not just "word" — the claim that the world is something reason can actually get a grip on.

English inherits *logos* mostly as invisible scaffolding — buried in "biology," "psychology," "epistemology" — without carrying over what the word originally staked a claim about. Before it meant "the study of," it meant something closer to "the rational order a thing can be given an account of."

Heraclitus, in the 6th century BC, was the first philosopher to make *logos* a technical term: the common rational structure underlying a constantly changing cosmos, which most people live "as if asleep" to, failing to notice even though it's "common to all." The Stoics later extended this into a cosmic governing principle (*logos spermatikos*), and the term migrated into rhetoric (Aristotle's appeal to reasoned argument, one of his [[Lexicon/persuasion-pillars|three pillars of persuasion]] alongside ethos and pathos) and later Christian theology (the Gospel of John's opening line). All these senses share Heraclitus's original wager: that reality is structured in a way reasoned discourse can actually track, not merely describe from outside.

*Source: Johnstone, M.A. (2014). "On 'Logos' in Heraclitus." Oxford Studies in Ancient Philosophy, 47, 1–30. See also Hussey, E. (1982). "Epistemology and Meaning in Heraclitus," in Language and Logos (Cambridge University Press).*
