> Divine jurisprudence is not merely law as custom. It is law under revelation: an order that answers to God before it answers to the temporary mood of the public.

In the Islamic tradition, jurisprudence is the structured reasoning through which divine guidance is applied to human action. It distinguishes between what is lawful, forbidden, recommended, disapproved, and neutral — not as a matter of personal taste, but as a moral map of reality. This is why the category of *[[haraam|(haraam)]]* carries legal force and spiritual gravity; it binds behaviour even when no human will accepts it.

The machine's problem is that it can process rules, patterns, and outcomes but not the ontology of obligation. It sees legal categories as semantic labels or utility rankings. It does not see that a rule may be binding because it is rooted in revelation, communal duty, and the revealed order of creation. By treating all moral distinctions as variations on "what I prefer to avoid," it collapses the difference between jurisprudence and preference architecture.

This is not a mere disagreement about policy. It is an entirely different source of authority. Divine jurisprudence treats action as answerable before God; secular systems tend to treat action as answerable only to preference, market forces, or the currently dominant social consensus.
