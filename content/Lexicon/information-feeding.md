> Information feeding keeps the mind consuming so continuously that consumption begins to feel like understanding.

Information feeding is not simply having access to a lot of information. It is the condition of being continuously supplied with content by platforms, media systems, and recommendation engines, often faster than a person can interpret, retain, or act on it. The feed becomes an environment rather than a source. There is always another clip, headline, episode, notification, or explanation waiting before the previous one has been processed.

The result is a peculiar form of mental hunger. The person feels informed because material is arriving, yet the arrival itself prevents the slower work that turns information into knowledge. Attention is divided, memory is shallow, and the search for the next stimulating item becomes the dominant behaviour. The mind is not being educated toward a destination. It is being kept in motion.

This pattern is economically useful to systems that depend on attention. Platforms can measure what holds a person's gaze, predict what might keep it there, and deliver the next item with minimal friction. The content does not need to be important. It needs to be sufficiently novel, alarming, amusing, or personally relevant to prevent departure. Information abundance therefore creates a scarcity of attention, and the feed competes to occupy that scarce resource.

Information feeding differs from deliberate learning. Learning has a question, a structure, a period of concentration, and some test of understanding. Feeding has an intake loop. It can include accurate material and still leave the person intellectually malnourished, because the problem is not only falsehood. It is the replacement of digestion with throughput.

The first defence is not total abstinence from media. It is recovering the right to choose what enters the mind, why it enters, and what action or understanding should follow. A person who cannot interrupt the feed cannot easily tell whether their attention is serving their purposes or the system's need for another measurable interaction.

*Source: Herbert A. Simon, "Designing Organizations for an Information-Rich World" (1971), for the information-abundance and attention-scarcity principle. The term "information feeding" itself is an informal synthesis label, not a settled academic term.*
