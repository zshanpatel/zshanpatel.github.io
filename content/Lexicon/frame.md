> The frame is the unspoken assertion of an experienced outcome. The alpha is the one who bears the scars of this outcome.

When frames collapse into debate, something essential is lost. The conversation becomes an exercise in egalitarian performance rather than a movement toward decision. Words multiply. Responsibility diffuses. Nobody bears the cost of being wrong because nobody commits to being right.

In sociology, Erving Goffman's Frame Analysis (1974) established the canonical definition: frames are the basic elements of organisation that govern events and our subjective involvement in them. Goffman drew from Gregory Bateson's 1955 "A Theory of Play and Fantasy," where the term "frame" was first used to distinguish play from serious activity. Goffman's contribution was to show that all social reality is framed, and that understanding what is happening in any situation requires identifying which frame is operating.

In the context of masculine social organisation, the frame functions differently from Goffman's analytical tool. Here, the frame is not merely how participants make sense of events. It is the assertion of an experienced outcome by the person who has borne its cost. The frame-holder is recognised by other men through scar-tested authority, not self-proclamation. Higher ambition is discussed, not debated. Words are weighted and used. The conversation concludes when there is a decision, not when everyone feels heard.
