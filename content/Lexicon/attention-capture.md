> Attention capture turns the mind's limited capacity to notice into an extractable resource.

The modern information environment does not merely contain more messages. It is organised around competing attempts to enter your awareness before you have chosen what deserves it. A headline, alert, autoplay sequence, or personalised advertisement wins its first battle when it interrupts the mind's current direction.

Attention capture is the deliberate use of salience, relevance, novelty, emotion, timing, and repetition to secure that interruption. Salience is not the same as importance. A message can be made prominent because it is alarming, exciting, or personally tailored, even when its substance does not deserve the attention it receives.

## The Scarce Resource

Herbert Simon described the structural problem plainly: a wealth of information creates a poverty of attention. Attention is limited by time, cognitive capacity, and the impossibility of giving full focus to competing demands at once. In an advertising-funded system, this limitation becomes an economic opportunity. Platforms and advertisers capture attention, measure the response, and sell access to the resulting audience.

The extraction is strongest when the person believes the choice was entirely their own. Personalisation makes the interruption feel relevant. Variable rewards make it worth checking again. Emotional intensity makes the message difficult to ignore. The system does not need to control every belief. It only needs to keep deciding what arrives first, what feels urgent, and what is repeatedly placed in front of the mind.

Attention capture therefore precedes persuasion. Before a message can change what someone thinks or buys, it must first become the thing they are looking at.

*Source: Simon, H.A. (1971), "Designing Organizations for an Information-Rich World"; Chardon-Boucaud, S. (2025), "The Attention Economy in the Digital Age," French Treasury, institutional analysis.*
