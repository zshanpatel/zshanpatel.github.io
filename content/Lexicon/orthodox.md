> The line between "true belief" and "heresy" is not discovered fully formed. It is drawn, by someone, at some point, and then defended as if it had always been there.

Every tradition of thought eventually accumulates a body of doctrine treated not as one interpretation among others but as the standard itself, the fixed point against which every other claim is measured and, if it deviates too far, declared wrong. That standard is orthodoxy. The word comes from the Greek *orthodoxos*, joining *orthos* ("right" or "straight") with *doxa* ("opinion" or "belief"), and entered English in the early 17th century meaning, literally, "right belief."

Orthodoxy performs a genuinely necessary function for any tradition that wants to persist across generations. It supplies doctrinal continuity, a stable core that new members can be taught and that keeps a community recognizably the same community over time. It also supplies communal identity: knowing what "we" believe is inseparable from knowing what distinguishes "us" from other groups, and orthodoxy is the mechanism that draws that boundary.

The complication, well established in religious studies rather than a fringe or purely polemical claim, is that orthodoxy is not a timeless given waiting to be identified. It is a historically contingent category, produced by specific institutions at specific moments, usually in the course of a real contest over doctrine in which one position eventually prevailed and then retroactively cast itself as having been the original, correct belief all along, with its rivals recast as later corruptions or "heresies." The German scholar Walter Bauer made this case most influentially for early Christianity in 1934, arguing that theological diversity, not a single unified orthodoxy, was the actual starting condition in many regions, and that "orthodoxy" and "heresy" as we use the terms are the vocabulary of the eventual winners. Subsequent scholarship has refined and contested the specifics of Bauer's account, but the broader point, that orthodoxy is made rather than simply inherited, has become a standard working assumption across the study of religion, not only in Christianity. Islamic intellectual history shows a parallel pattern: what came to be treated as Sunni theological orthodoxy crystallized gradually, through centuries of real argument among competing schools, rather than existing complete and settled from the outset.

None of this means orthodoxy is arbitrary or that its content doesn't matter. It means that calling a belief "orthodox" is itself a historical and institutional claim, not a neutral description, and that the boundary it draws was drawn by particular people, for particular reasons, at a particular time.
