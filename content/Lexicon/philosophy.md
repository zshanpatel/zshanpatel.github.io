> The subject that asks what the other subjects are for. It doesn't retire once science shows up, in fact science was one of its answers.

Philosophy's classical roots are in Athens: Plato asking what justice and knowledge really are, Aristotle building systematic logic and ethics underneath every field that came after. That conversation didn't end there, it moved. Islamic scholars preserved, translated, and extended it for centuries when much of it had been lost elsewhere, working under *falsafa* — the direct Arabic transliteration of the Greek term, not a separate native name, marking a real continuity rather than a parallel tradition: al-Ghazali's critique of pure philosophical certainty, Ibn Khaldun's foundational work on the philosophy of history and society, both pushing the same core questions: what can we actually know, and how should we live — into new territory rather than abandoning them.

The line runs forward from there. Adam Smith wrote as a moral philosopher before he wrote as an economist. *The Wealth of Nations* sits downstream of *The Theory of Moral Sentiments*, not apart from it. Contemporary continental philosophy still argues versions of the same questions applied to politics and civilisation, from mainstream liberal political theory to more contested traditionalist voices like Alexander Dugin, proof the conversation hasn't settled, whatever field claims to have replaced it. Philosophy as a discipline isn't nostalgia for a pre-scientific age; it's the ongoing argument about what any given field's answers are actually worth.

Source: Williams, B. *Philosophy as a Humanistic Discipline*, Princeton University Press, 2006; Rahman, H. "From Farabi to Ibn Khaldun: The Perception of State in the Early Muslim Intellectual's Writings," 2021; Gutas, D. *Greek Philosophers in the Arabic Tradition*, Routledge, 2020.
