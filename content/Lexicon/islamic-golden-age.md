> Knowledge doesn't survive collapse on its own — someone has to carry it across the gap.

After the fall of Rome, a large share of Greek philosophical and scientific texts survived not primarily in Europe but through a sustained, state-supported translation and research effort across the Abbasid Caliphate, centred on Baghdad. Scholars there translated Greek, Persian, and Indian sources into Arabic, then extended them — in mathematics, astronomy, medicine, and philosophy — producing original work that later fed directly into the European Renaissance's own recovery of classical learning.

Historians of science treat this translation movement as one of history's genuine large-scale knowledge-transmission events, not simply a passive holding pattern between Greece and Europe: the period's scholars critiqued, developed, and in places surpassed the Greek sources they inherited.

*Source: Gutas, D. (1998). Greek Thought, Arabic Culture: The Graeco-Arabic Translation Movement in Baghdad and Early Abbasid Society. Routledge. Cross-referenced against Wujastyk, D. (2016). "From Balkh to Baghdad: Indian Science and the Birth of the Islamic Golden Age in the Eighth Century." Indian Journal of History of Science, 51(4).*
