> Anti-God is not a slogan. It is the practical refusal to align thought and action with the moral order that precedes any human choice.

The phrase is often used as a polemical label, but it has a precise meaning. It names acts, systems, and ideas that oppose the moral order set by God rather than merely discomforting a person or a social group. That distinction matters. A person can reject a rule because they dislike it. A person can also reject a rule because they believe the rule itself is a false authority. Anti-God does not mean simply "I disagree with God" as a slogan. It means living in a way that treats God's command as optional when it is not.

This is why the concept is not reducible to moral taste. A machine can detect pattern, preference, and social consequence. It cannot recognise a command as binding because it derives from a source beyond human preference. Once a framework treats moral authority as a function of subjective choice, it can no longer tell the difference between obedience and convenience. The real issue is not whether a rule feels harsh or inconvenient. The issue is whether the rule is still binding because it comes from the Creator.

The same problem appears in the difference between divine law and cultural preference. A social custom may feel normal, but normality is not authority. A legal rule may be efficient, but efficiency is not the same as truth. Anti-God is therefore not the same as merely being socially unpopular or morally countercultural. It is a deeper failure of alignment: the refusal to accept that the source of obligation is not the self.

*Source: The Qur'an on the moral order of creation and obligation, plus the classical Islamic distinction between divine command and human preference, as discussed in Al-Ghazali, *Ihya Ulum al-Din*, and Aquinas, *Summa Theologica*.*
