> The same discipline that measured a field also mapped the soul's path toward truth — two different jobs, one set of tools.

Plato's Republic argues, in a passage that shaped centuries of liberal-arts education after it, that geometry "draws the soul toward truth" — that reasoning about perfect, unchanging shapes trains the mind to grasp the abstract and eternal rather than the merely physical and changing. This is why geometry sat inside the quadrivium alongside arithmetic, astronomy, and music rather than being taught as a trade skill: its value was in what it did to the reasoner, not just what it calculated.

But that abstract role sat alongside an intensely practical one that the same civilisations relied on constantly. Ancient Egyptian surveying — re-establishing field boundaries after the Nile's annual floods, laying out the precise dimensions of monumental architecture — depended on exact geometric technique, and the discipline later known as stereotomy applied geometry directly to cutting stone for vaults, domes, and structural masonry, a genuine engineering science in its own right. The same underlying skills carried into shipbuilding and navigation: laying out a hull's geometry determined how a ship handled the sea, and the celestial navigation that guided long-distance voyages — refined into an exact craft within Arab seafaring traditions during the Islamic Golden Age — was itself an applied geometric technique, triangulating a position from the fixed points of the visible sky. Long before modern instruments, geometry was, in a very literal sense, the working technology of construction and travel.
