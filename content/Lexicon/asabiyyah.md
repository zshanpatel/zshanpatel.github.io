> Power does not originate in wealth, ideology, or formal title. It originates in the willingness of a group to act as one — and to endure in order to do so.

Ibn Khaldun identified *asabiyyah* in the *Muqaddimah* (1377) as the fundamental bond of human society and the primary motive force of history. Usually translated as group solidarity or social cohesion, it is more precisely the capacity of a group to subordinate individual comfort to a shared cause — to bear collective hardship, to coordinate under pressure, and to trust one another enough to act without hesitation.

This is not sentiment. It is the precondition of all durable power *[[power|(power)]]*. Armies, institutions, and economies are downstream of it.

## How It Forms and How It Dies

Asabiyyah is forged at the margins, not the centre. It emerges where survival is genuinely collective — where the group is the reason any individual within it is still alive. Nomadic tribes, frontier communities, persecuted minorities, revolutionary movements: these are its natural crucibles. The discipline is not imposed; it is earned through shared necessity.

Its trajectory follows a pattern Ibn Khaldun observed across Arab, Berber, and Persian dynasties without exception: the cohesive group rises, displaces the comfortable ruling class, establishes its own dynasty — and then begins to dissolve under the very conditions its success creates. Luxury enters. Individual interests diverge. The solidarity that was once structural becomes merely rhetorical. The dynasty still exists; the asabiyyah that built it does not.

Ibn Khaldun wrote that dynasties rarely survive beyond three generations of roughly forty years each. The first generation builds in full possession of the original cohesion. The second inherits the structure without the formative struggle. The third mistakes the inheritance for the achievement. By the fourth, a new group with stronger asabiyyah is already assembling at the periphery — usually dismissed, until it is not.

## What It Means Beyond Dynasties

The concept extends far beyond medieval Arab politics. Any organisation, movement, or institution that has lost its founding asabiyyah will recognise the symptoms: coordination that now requires enforcement, leadership that now requires performance, loyalty that now requires incentive. The form holds; the force does not.

Restoring asabiyyah cannot be done through strategy documents or cultural initiatives. It requires the conditions that produce it — shared stakes, genuine interdependence, and a cause worth the cost. Which is why the most powerful question any group can ask is not "how do we communicate our values?" but "do we still share a reason to act as one?"
