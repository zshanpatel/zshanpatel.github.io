> Epistemic bias is the hidden framework that decides what can count as knowledge before evidence is even weighed.

Most arguments about bias begin too late. They ask whether a conclusion is distorted, while leaving unexamined the prior assumptions that decided which questions mattered, which evidence counted, and who could speak with authority.

Epistemic bias is the patterned influence of a worldview, method, institution, or inherited category on the production and evaluation of knowledge. It is not limited to personal prejudice or a mistaken judgment. A framework can be internally consistent and still be biased because it makes some forms of experience visible while treating others as irrelevant, irrational, or outside the field of knowledge.

## Framework Before Conclusion

The distinction matters. [[confirmation-bias|Confirmation bias]] operates within a person's reasoning by favouring evidence that supports an existing belief. Epistemic bias operates at a deeper level. It shapes the standards by which evidence is selected, classified, interpreted, and authorised in the first place.

That does not mean every framework is equally arbitrary. It means that claims of neutrality require examination. A secular framework, a religious framework, a scientific discipline, and a colonial institution each carry assumptions about reality, authority, evidence, and value. [[epistemology|Epistemology]] asks how knowledge is justified. Epistemic bias asks what a framework has already excluded or privileged while answering that question.

The remedy is not to pretend that a view from nowhere is possible. It is to make the framework visible, test its exclusions, and compare its account of reality against competing sources of knowledge. For a revelation-centred account, this includes asking whether reason has been severed from revelation and whether inherited categories have mistaken their own limits for the limits of truth.

*Source: Academic grounding includes Sandra Harding, Whose Science? Whose Knowledge? (1991), and Miranda Fricker, Epistemic Injustice (2007). The term is used here as a framework-level synthesis, not as a claim that one standard definition is settled.*
