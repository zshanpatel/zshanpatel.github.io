> Behavioural psychology explains action by tracing what the environment teaches, rewards, punishes, and repeats.

Human behaviour is often treated as the direct expression of personality or intention. That view misses the machinery around the person: the cues, routines, rewards, and consequences that make some actions easier to repeat than others.

Behavioural psychology, also called behaviorism, developed as an attempt to study psychology through observable behaviour and measurable relationships with the environment. Its central mechanisms are classical conditioning, where a response becomes associated with a stimulus, and operant conditioning, where the consequences of an action strengthen or weaken the likelihood that it will recur.

The approach made behaviour legible. A reward can reinforce an action, while an unpleasant consequence can suppress it. This logic appears in education, therapy, workplace design, advertising, and digital products. Its original strictness also created its limit: a psychology focused only on observable action can understate the role of thought, emotion, interpretation, and meaning. Modern behavioural science therefore uses the behavioural insight without treating the inner life as irrelevant.

*Source: OpenStax, “The Behavioral Perspective: Classical and Operant Conditioning,” Pressbooks.*
