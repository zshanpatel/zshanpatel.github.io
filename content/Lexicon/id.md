> The most primitive layer of the psyche, demanding immediate gratification without negotiation or restraint.

Most of us experience desires that seem to arrive unbidden: hunger, sexuality, anger, the urge to possess or consume something we see. A modern person interprets these as "just what humans want" and concludes either that satisfying them is natural and should be pursued, or that controlling them requires constant vigilance against one's "true nature." Both readings assume the same thing: that underneath the social layer lies an undisciplined animal wanting what it wants.

Freud's id names this layer precisely. In his structural model (introduced in *The Ego and the Id*, 1923), the id is the sum of all instinctual drives and unconscious impulses, operating according to what Freud called the pleasure principle: a demand for immediate gratification regardless of external constraint or internal morality. It has no sense of time, consequence, or social appropriateness. It cannot reason or plan. It simply wants.

This description resonates partly because it names a real phenomenon many recognize in their own psychology: the presence of impulses, urges, and desires that feel like they come from somewhere outside conscious intention. Yet the universality of Freud's model is disputed. Alternative psychological traditions treat the corresponding layer differently. In Islamic psychology, the *[[Lexicon/nafs|nafs]]* (lower self) occupies a parallel structural position as the seat of raw desire, but Islamic frameworks do not treat the raw drive itself as the problem requiring suppression. Instead, they distinguish between raw desire left undisciplined and that same energy channeled toward higher purposes through a process called *tazkiyat al-nafs* (purification of the self). The material to be refined exists. The verdict on what it is for changes.

*Source: Freud, S. (1923). The Ego and the Id.*
