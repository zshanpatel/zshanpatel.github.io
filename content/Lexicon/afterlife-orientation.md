> The afterlife is not an escape from life. It is the horizon that gives life its moral weight.

Modern life trains people to treat the present as the only serious reality. Success is measured by what can be accumulated, displayed, or experienced now. That compression of time turns morality into preference and makes the future answerable to immediate appetite.

An afterlife orientation reverses that order. In Islamic thought, worldly life is temporary and human action remains accountable beyond death. The point is not to despise the world, withdraw from work, or refuse lawful enjoyment. It is to judge those things by whether they serve a faithful return to God.

This is a personal moral horizon, not a complete theory of the end times. It differs from [[dunya|dunya]], which names worldly life itself, and from [[eschatological-framework|eschatological framework]], which is an analytical lens for reading civilisational change. Afterlife orientation operates at the level of daily intention, restraint, work, wealth, and responsibility.

It changes the question from “What can I get away with?” to “What am I taking with me?” That question makes consequence larger than reputation and purpose larger than self-optimisation.

Source: Qur'an 57:20 and 28:77; Yaqoob and Batool, “The Concept of the Afterlife in the Qur'an,” *Journal of Religion and Society* 4, no. 1 (2025), web-accessible secondary source whose peer-review status was not independently verified here.
