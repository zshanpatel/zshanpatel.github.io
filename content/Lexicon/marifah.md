> Knowledge that changes the knower, not knowledge the knower merely holds.

Most theories of knowledge treat knowing as something accumulated — facts added to a store, propositions confirmed true one at a time. Ma'rifah names a different claim: that some knowledge is not accumulated but received, and that receiving it changes what the recipient is, not just what they know.

In the Sufi tradition, ma'rifah is distinguished from *[[Lexicon/ilm|'ilm]]* (learned, propositional knowledge) and *[[Lexicon/aql|'aql]]* (rational inference) — not opposed to them, but positioned as a further stage reached through spiritual discipline rather than study alone. Al-Ghazali described it as arising when rational knowledge, pursued far enough through disciplined practice, opens onto direct experiential understanding. Ibn 'Arabi framed it instead through an "imaginal realm" that mediates between the seeker and ultimate meaning. Both treat it as inherently transformative — reshaping the knower rather than adding an item to an inventory.

Recent scholarship reads ma'rifah as an integrated epistemology uniting epistemic, ethical, and ontological dimensions — knowledge, virtue, and the restoration of the self treated as a single movement rather than three separate achievements. *Source: Wawaysadhya & Andri Azis Putra, "Ma'rifah as an Epistemology of Transformation: A Philosophical Reading of Sufism in al-Ghazali and Ibn 'Arabi," Matsnawi: Journal of Tasawwuf and Psychotherapy Studies (2026) — a regional peer-reviewed Islamic-studies venue, noted honestly rather than as a top-tier general philosophy journal.*
