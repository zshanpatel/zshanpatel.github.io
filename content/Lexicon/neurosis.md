> Neurosis is the ego's habitual defence against its own conflicts, producing a state of chronic dysfunction that never becomes acute enough to force a crisis.

Modern life is full of people who function well enough to avoid consequences but not well enough to actually change. They hold jobs, maintain relationships, and navigate daily routines, yet something remains perpetually stuck. The problem is not dramatic collapse. It is the quiet, ongoing avoidance of whatever would need to be confronted for real transformation to occur. This is the territory Freud's concept of neurosis was built to describe.

In psychoanalytic theory, neurosis arises when the ego deploys defence mechanisms, such as repression, displacement, or rationalisation, to manage conflicts between unconscious drives and social or moral demands. The result is not psychosis, which breaks with reality, but something subtler: a persistent, low-level dysfunction where anxiety is managed but never resolved, symptoms appear but never escalate, and the person remains just functional enough to never be forced to reckon with what they are avoiding. Freud (1917) described it as the ego's failure to allocate its own drives, producing symptoms that are as burdensome as the conflict they were meant to resolve. The DSM-II (1968) formalised this as anxiety controlled unconsciously through conversion, displacement, and other mechanisms, producing subjective distress without gross personality disorganisation.

The danger of neurosis is precisely its stability. Unlike a catastrophic failure, which forces awareness and creates the possibility of change, neurosis maintains itself through invisible micro-adjustments. The person never hits a bottom because the bottom keeps receding, absorbed into an ever-thinning layer of coping. It is a forever jail: one you do not understand or even know you are in.

*Source: Freud, S. (1917). Introductory Lectures on Psychoanalysis. DSM-II (1968).*
