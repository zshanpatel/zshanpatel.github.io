> Reasoned interpretation of the law belongs to the community's conscience, not to a narrow class of gatekeepers.

Debates about political Islam are usually posed as a binary: theocracy governed by a clerical class, or secular democracy that excludes religion from public reasoning altogether. *[[muhammad-iqbal|Muhammad Iqbal]]*, in his 1930 lecture series *The Reconstruction of Religious Thought in Islam*, rejected both options and argued for a third model he named spiritual democracy.

Iqbal held that the "ultimate aim of Islam" is to build a spiritual democracy, a political order that neither privatises religion nor concentrates its interpretation permanently in the hands of a scholarly elite. His argument rests on *[[tawhid|tawhid]]*, the oneness of God, which he read as carrying a social corollary: since only God holds ultimate authority, no human class, including the traditional ulema, can claim an exclusive, unquestionable monopoly on interpreting divine law. He called *[[ijtihad|ijtihad]]*, independent legal reasoning, the *[[principle-of-movement|principle of movement]]* in the structure of Islam, and argued that this reasoning should shift from individual jurists working in isolation toward an elected legislative assembly, exercised through *ijma* (collective consensus) and answerable to the community's own considered judgment.

This is why "democracy" is the operative word: authority is meant to circulate through the collective conscience of the *[[ummah|ummah]]* rather than sit permanently with a narrow gatekeeping class. And "spiritual" is not decorative. Iqbal explicitly rejected the liberal move of separating a "neutral" public sphere from a private religious one, arguing that the state itself is "only an effort to realise the spiritual in a human organisation," so that material and political questions carry the same religious weight as questions of ritual. He also acknowledged the risk in his own proposal: representatives untrained in Islamic law could legislate carelessly, which is why he paired wider participation with a demand for genuine religious education, not participation for its own sake.

*Source: Muhammad Iqbal, The Reconstruction of Religious Thought in Islam (1930), Lecture VI, "The Principle of Movement in the Structure of Islam."*
