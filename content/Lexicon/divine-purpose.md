> Divine purpose gives life a direction beyond the self: to act in the world as a servant and steward while preparing to return to God.

The modern question of purpose is often reduced to preference. People are asked what they want to become, what experiences they want to collect, or what legacy they want to leave. These questions may organise ambition, but they do not establish why a human life exists or what makes one direction better than another.

Divine purpose begins from a different order of reference. Life is not an accidental interval whose meaning each person must invent from private desire. It is a trust with a source, a task, and an end. The human being acts within the world, but the world is not the final court of judgment. Personal gifts, work, relationships, and material responsibility become meaningful when they are directed toward what God requires and toward the final return to Him.

This is not withdrawal from ordinary life. Divine purpose gives ordinary action greater weight because choices become part of an answerable life. Work can become service, strength can become protection, wealth can become circulation and care, and knowledge can become a means of clearer obedience. The concept therefore differs from [[eschatological-framework|eschatological framework]], which is an analytical lens for reading historical transition. Divine purpose is the orientation that governs a person's action within that history.

It also differs from self-improvement. Development is not rejected, but subordinated. The question is not simply whether the self has become more capable. It is whether capability has been placed in the right direction, under [[deen|deen]], [[tawakkul|tawakkul]], and a living relation to [[divine|the Divine]]. The horizon of purpose is completed by final accountability: the life being built now is also the life that will be answered for.

*Source: Qur'an 23:115, as translated and presented by Quran.com, rejects the idea that creation is purposeless and joins human life to return to God. Encyclopaedia Britannica, "Eschatology," describes Islamic judgment in terms of divine judgment based on records of deeds.*
