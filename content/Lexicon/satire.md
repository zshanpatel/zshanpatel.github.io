> Satire is the art of saying the wrong thing so clearly that the right thing becomes impossible to ignore.

Most communication aims to be understood on its face. Satire does the opposite: it says something it does not mean in order to reveal what everyone is already doing but refusing to see. The mode works by holding a mirror up to a belief or behaviour, but a funhouse mirror, one that exaggerates the shape just enough to make the familiar grotesque. A reader who takes satire literally has failed not the author but themselves. The misreading is the proof that the target exists.

The word derives from the Latin *satura*, meaning a mixed platter, a dish of varied ingredients, and was later confused with the Greek *satyr*, the rough, sexually aggressive woodland creature, which gave the genre its reputation for coarseness. Roman poets Horace and Juvenal established its two enduring registers: Horatian satire, which mocks gently and with affection, and Juvenalian satire, which attacks with contempt and anger. Both share the same structural requirement identified by Northrop Frye: satire demands at least a token fantasy, a content recognisable as grotesque, and at least an implicit moral standard. Without the moral standard, it is merely insult. Without the grotesque exaggeration, it is merely criticism.

The modern challenge is that satire requires an audience capable of detecting irony, a capacity that weakens when public discourse flattens nuance into literal takes and tribal signalling. When everything is processed at face value, the satirist's tool becomes a liability: the provocation lands as endorsement, the exaggeration as sincerity, and the critique as the thing it was attacking. This is not a failure of the genre. It is a diagnostic of the reading culture. A society that cannot distinguish between "do this" and "look how absurd it is to do this" has lost the literacy that satire depends on, and with it, one of the oldest mechanisms for holding power to account without violence.
