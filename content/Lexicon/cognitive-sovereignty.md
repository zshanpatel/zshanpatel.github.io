> The question isn't whether a tool can think for you. It's whether you can still tell the difference between your reasoning and its residue.

A person can use an AI system to think through a problem and walk away with a confident answer without ever having actually reasoned through it themselves. Because the process still feels like thinking, there is often no internal signal that anything was outsourced at all.

The term builds on an older, established concept: cognitive liberty, introduced by legal scholars Wrye Sententia and Richard Glen Boire in 2000 as an update to the historical right to "freedom of thought," adapted for an era of neurotechnology and defined as the right to control one's own mental processes free of external manipulation or intrusion. Cognitive sovereignty narrows this further, for the specific problem of habitual AI-assisted cognition. Philosopher Amir Konigsberg's 2026 formulation defines it as the capacity to notice when one's thinking is being displaced, to maintain a meaningful connection to how one's beliefs and judgments are actually formed, and to distinguish genuine reasoning from the mere subjective feeling of having reasoned.

This is distinct from related ideas like critical thinking or metacognition. Those describe evaluating a conclusion's quality; cognitive sovereignty, per Konigsberg's framing, is specifically about authorship, whether the reasoning process that produced a belief was actually one's own.

*Source: Sententia, W. & Boire, R.G. (2000), founding statement of the Center for Cognitive Liberty and Ethics; Konigsberg, A. (2026), "Cognitive Sovereignty: The Authorship Problem in AI-Assisted Thought," SSRN preprint, not yet peer-reviewed.*
