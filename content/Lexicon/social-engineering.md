> Some engineering builds bridges. This kind builds — or reshapes — the people crossing them.

Long before "social engineering" became shorthand for phishing emails and pretext phone calls, the term named something broader in political philosophy: Karl Popper's idea of "piecemeal social engineering," incremental, testable institutional reform aimed at solving specific social problems, as opposed to sweeping utopian schemes imposed all at once. Popper defended a cautious, evidence-driven version of the idea, while critics like Friedrich Hayek warned that even piecemeal intervention carries real risks of unintended consequences.

The essay-level, persuasion-adjacent sense of the phrase — the coordinated shaping of what people believe using credibility, logic, and emotion together — sits closer to this older, structural meaning than to the narrower cybersecurity usage: it's about engineering outcomes in a population, not tricking one individual into clicking a link.

*Source: Simkin, C. (1993). "Popper and Hayek on Piecemeal Social Engineering." In Popper's Views on Natural and Social Science. Brill.*
